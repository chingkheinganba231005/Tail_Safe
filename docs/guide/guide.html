How to use this guide

This guide explains TailSafe from the very beginning. You do not need to know anything about fire safety, statistics or computers. Every new word is explained the first time it appears, and the word list at the end explains them all again.

TailSafe in one minute
  1. People leave tall buildings by the stairs. When a fire starts at night, many people are asleep, some are old or use a wheelchair, and a staircase can fill with smoke.
  2. On most nights everyone gets out in good time. On a few bad nights it takes much, much longer. Those few bad nights are the ones that matter.
  3. TailSafe plays out hundreds or thousands of possible nights on a computer, each one a little different.
  4. It shows how bad the worst nights are, who is still inside, and what causes it.
  5. Then it tests cheap changes, such as using lifts for people in wheelchairs or asking neighbours to knock on doors, and tells you honestly what helps and what makes things worse.

How the guide is organised

  • Part 2, How TailSafe works: what happens inside, one idea at a time, with pictures and no maths.

  • Part 5, Putting it together: a worked example, the command line, the limits, troubleshooting, questions and the word list.

Chapter 1

Tall buildings, fires and people

Many people in Hong Kong live very high up. When there is a fire, almost all of them have to walk down the stairs.

Hong Kong lives up high

Hong Kong has a great many tall homes. Public housing blocks and private towers of 30 to 50 storeys, and more, are ordinary. Well over a thousand people can live in one building, and they share only a few staircases, often just two.

The building this guide uses as its main example is a 40-storey public housing block shaped like a cross. In TailSafe's model it has 760 flats, 2 staircases (called Stair A and Stair B), 3 lifts, and on a Sunday night at 3 a.m. about 1,825 residents at home. The building and its residents are made up by TailSafe; they are not a real block.

How people leave a tall building in a fire

An evacuation (everyone leaving a building because of danger) happens in a few stages:

  1. The alarm sounds.
  2. People notice and decide to go. People who are awake notice quickly. People who are asleep take much longer. The time between the alarm and starting to move is called the pre-movement time.
  3. They get ready. They wake the family, find shoes and keys, and help a grandparent out of bed.
  4. They walk to a staircase along the corridor, sometimes through a lobby.
  5. They walk down, floor after floor. On a long staircase people get tired and slow down, and queues form where the staircase is full.
  6. They leave through an exit on the ground floor.

The lifts are normally not used to escape. In Hong Kong they are kept for the firefighters. One lift in every building is the firefighting lift, which only firefighters use. So almost everyone must use the stairs.

Some people cannot use the stairs on their own, for example a person in a wheelchair. In TailSafe, such a person is carried down by their family or a live-in domestic helper if someone able is at home. Otherwise they wait for the firefighters to rescue them. Rescue can take a long time, because firefighters climb the stairs and bring people down one household at a time.

Many tall buildings have refuge floors: an open floor, every so many storeys, where people can step out of the staircase into fresher air and rest before going on.

In plain words

A fire in a tall building is a slow walk down a narrow staircase, shared with hundreds of neighbours, some of whom are asleep, old or unable to walk.

Chapter 2

The race against smoke

Every fire is a race between two clocks: how long a place stays safe, and how long people need to leave it.

In a building fire, the smoke is often more dangerous than the flames. Smoke does two things. It makes the air dark, so you cannot see the way. And it is poisonous: breathing it makes people weak and confused, and in the end they cannot move. When a place has become too dangerous to be in, it is called untenable.

Fire engineers describe the race with two clocks:

  • ASET, the available safe egress time: how long a place stays safe before smoke makes it untenable. Think of it as the smoke clock. (Egress simply means going out.)
  • RSET, the required safe egress time: how long the people there need to get out. Think of it as the walking clock.

If the walking clock is shorter than the smoke clock, everyone leaves in time. If the walking clock is longer, someone is still there when it has become dangerous. TailSafe writes this as RSET > ASET (“RSET is bigger than ASET”) and counts how often it happens.

In TailSafe, a place becomes untenable at the first moment one of these is true:

  • you can see less than 10 metres through the smoke;
  • the air is hotter than 60 °C;
  • a person who had stood there since the fire started would already have breathed a harmful dose of smoke. (Chapter 11 explains the dose.)

These limits follow ISO 13571, an international guideline on how long people can survive in a fire. The TailSafe settings file notes that the exact values still have to be checked against the edition of the guideline in use.

In plain words

ASET is how long you have. RSET is how long you need. Trouble starts when you need more time than you have.

Chapter 3

Why the average is not enough

This is the most important idea in TailSafe, and the reason for its name.

A story about buses

Think about your trip to school or work. Most days it takes 30 minutes. Some days 35. But now and then the bus breaks down and the trip takes an hour and a half. If someone asks how long your trip takes, the average (perhaps 33 minutes) is a fine answer for planning an ordinary day. But if you must never, ever be late for an important exam, the average is no use at all. You need to know about the bad days.

Fires are the same. A building checked only against an average night can look fine, while a few bad nights are very bad. And fires do not choose average nights: they happen at 3 a.m., when a staircase is full of smoke and a lift is broken.

The long tail

Imagine the same fire night happening 300 times, each time a little differently: different people at home, a different flat on fire, the smoke arriving at a different moment. Draw a bar for each of the 300 nights, from the quickest evacuation to the slowest:

The same 300 nights can be drawn another way, as a histogram: the nights are sorted into boxes by how long they took, and each bar shows how many nights landed in each box. Most nights pile up in the middle. A thin trail of slow nights stretches out to the right. That trail is called the tail, and it is where TailSafe gets its name.

Four ways to describe the spread

NameWhat it meansIn the example
Mean (average)Add up the times of all nights and divide by the number of nights.136.5 min
MedianThe night in the middle: half the nights are quicker, half are slower.131.6 min
P95 (95th percentile)95 nights in every 100 are quicker than this. It is the border of the worst 5%.199.5 min
CVaR95Take the worst 5% of nights (here the worst 15 of 300) and average them. It answers: when things go badly, how badly, on average?218.1 min

CVaR95 is short for conditional value at risk at 95%. The name comes from banks, which use it to ask “on our worst days, how much could we lose?”. TailSafe borrows the idea and asks “on the worst nights, how long does it take?”. In this guide it is often called simply the average of the worst 5%.

Why not simply look at the single worst night? Because one night can be a freak. The average of the worst 5% is steadier, and still tells you about the bad nights.

Remember

TailSafe cares most about CVaR95, the average of the worst 5% of nights, because the bad nights are where people get hurt. When it tests a change, it asks first how much the change shortens the bad nights, not the average one.

Chapter 4

Who is most at risk

Not everyone is equally likely to be among the last people out.

  • People who are asleep react later. At night, the time before people start to move is much longer.
  • Older residents (65 to 79) walk more slowly, especially down many flights of stairs.
  • Frail older residents (80 and over) walk very slowly and tire quickly.
  • Wheelchair users cannot walk down. They must be carried, or wait for a lift or for the firefighters.
  • People on high floors have the longest way down, and often meet queues on the way.
  • Families move together, so a family is only as fast as its slowest member.
  • Some households first go the wrong way: to fetch a relative or to check on a neighbour, walking against everyone else. This is called counter-flow.

Hong Kong's population is ageing, so the slowest groups are growing. In TailSafe, some households also have a live-in domestic helper, who can help an older person move or carry a wheelchair user down.

What the example shows

In the 1,000-night study of the 40-storey example (chapter 30):

  • Households with a wheelchair user living on 30/F to 39/F are only 1.2% of the residents, yet they are all of the people still inside at the very end of the worst nights, because they wait for the firefighters.
  • Among people who leave on their own, households with a frail resident aged 80 or over living on 30/F to 39/F are 4.9% of the residents but 70% of those still inside near the end of the worst nights.
In plain words

The worst nights are mostly about a small number of people: the old, the frail and wheelchair users on high floors. A good plan has to work for them.

Chapter 5

What TailSafe is, and what it is not

TailSafe is

  • a practice tool, like a flight simulator for evacuations;
  • a safe way to ask “what if?” without a real fire or a real drill;
  • a way to see where the risk comes from and who carries it;
  • a fair way to compare changes, on exactly the same nights.

TailSafe is not

  • a fire-safety assessment of any building;
  • a replacement for a registered fire engineer, the Fire Services Department, or the Buildings Department's Code of Practice for Fire Safety in Buildings;
  • a picture of any real residents: everyone in it is made up, and no personal data is used.

Many of the numbers inside TailSafe are educated guesses. TailSafe keeps every physical and population number in one settings file, config/params.yaml, and every number there says where it comes from. Of its 169 numbers, 146 are marked ASSUMPTION — needs citation: they are reasonable guesses that still need a proper source. The walking speed of frail residents, reaction times at night and how quickly firefighters rescue people are among them. The remaining numbers name a published source, which is still marked to be checked against the edition used.

That is why TailSafe is good for understanding and comparing (where does the risk come from? which change helps more?) and not for exact predictions (how many minutes will it take in my building?).

Watch out

Never say “TailSafe says our building is safe”. Say instead: “TailSafe suggests where the risk concentrates and which changes might help. A fire engineer should check this.”

The full responsible-use notice, which TailSafe shows on every screen and in every report, is in chapter 32.

Chapter 7

The building as a map

TailSafe does not see a building as a picture. It sees a map of places, and of the connections people use to move between them.

Places and connections

The places are flats, corridors, lobbies, stair landings, refuge areas and exits. The connections are the doors, the walkways and the stair flights between them. Every connection knows how long it is and how wide it is. Mathematicians call a map like this a graph (nothing to do with charts): the places are its nodes and the connections are its edges.

Doors and stairs are like pipes

Only so many people fit through a door or down a staircase each second, just as only so much water fits through a pipe. When more people arrive than can pass, the rest wait, and a queue forms, like at a supermarket checkout.

How many people can pass depends on the width. TailSafe uses a standard engineering method, the hydraulic model from the SFPE Handbook of Fire Protection Engineering. It says that a staircase lets roughly one person per second pass for each metre of usable width, and a door a little more. The usable width is a bit less than the real width, because people keep about 15 cm away from the wall on each side.

Here is why this matters so much. The staircases in the 40-storey example are 1.1 m wide. Take 15 cm off each side and 0.8 m is usable, which lets about 48 people a minute down. The block has about 1,825 residents at night. Just squeezing all of them through two such staircases takes about 19 minutes; through one staircase it takes about 38 minutes. So if smoke closes one staircase, the whole building has to squeeze through the other one.

Walking speed

People walk more slowly in a crowd, more slowly on stairs than on the flat, and more slowly still after many floors, because they get tired. Smoke slows them down too, because they cannot see well. TailSafe includes all four effects.

Taking turns on the stairs

On every floor, people coming out of the corridor have to squeeze into a staircase that is already carrying people from the floors above. TailSafe lets the two streams take turns in a fixed ratio, like cars merging where two lanes become one.

The four building types

TailSafe can build four kinds of Hong Kong building for you. They are generic designs made by the computer from a set of rules (TailSafe calls them procedural), not copies of real buildings.

CruciformA cross-shaped public housing block: four wings of flats around a central core with the lifts and two staircases. Standard size: 40 storeys.
SlabA long, straight block: one long corridor with flats on both sides, a staircase at each end and the lifts in the middle. Standard size: 30 storeys.
Twin coreA private residential tower on a podium (a wide base without homes). Its core holds a pair of interlocking scissor staircases. Standard size: 45 storeys.
Care homeA low-rise residential care home for the elderly, where many residents need help to move and staff help them. Standard size: 4 storeys.

A scissor staircase is two staircases twisted around each other in the same shaft, so that each floor can reach two separate escape routes. A storey count includes the ground floor.

Chapter 8

The people

TailSafe fills the building with made-up people, called synthetic people. No real person's data is ever used.

Six kinds of people

Kind of personWhat is special about them in TailSafe
Able adultWalks at a normal pace and can help others.
ChildMoves together with the family.
Older adult (65 to 79)Walks more slowly, especially down stairs, and tires sooner.
Frail older adult (80 and over)Walks very slowly and tires quickly. Waits for an evacuation lift when lifts are in use.
Wheelchair userCannot walk down stairs. Is carried down by the household if an able person is at home, waits for an evacuation lift if lifts are in use, or waits for the firefighters.
Domestic helperA live-in helper, who can help an older person move or carry a wheelchair user down.

For each kind of person TailSafe knows a walking speed on the flat, going down stairs and going up stairs, how quickly they tire, how long they usually take to react to the alarm, and how much room they take up. Speeds and reaction times are not fixed: every person gets their own, drawn from a range.

Households

Every flat gets a household. Its size depends on the size of the flat. The ages of its members are drawn so that, over the whole building, the share of residents aged 65 and over matches the setting you choose. Some households have a live-in helper. Some flats are empty.

Who is at home depends on the time. TailSafe knows four time slots: weekday day, weekday night, weekend day and weekend night. At night most people are at home and asleep; on a weekday afternoon many are out at work or school.

In a care home, residents live in rooms, and staff help them out: each member of staff escorts the residents of one room.

How people behave

  • Families stay together and move at the pace of their slowest member.
  • Some go against the flow first: a share of households visit another flat, on their own floor or a few floors up, to fetch family or check on a neighbour, before they leave.
  • Some rest on a refuge floor on the way down.
  • Everyone knows the quickest way out, and learns that a staircase is blocked only when they reach it. Then they turn around and use another one.
In plain words

Every night TailSafe invents a new set of neighbours for the building, with realistic ages, speeds and habits, and then watches how they get out.

Chapter 9

One night in the simulator

A simulator is a program that imitates something real. TailSafe's simulator plays out one fire night, second by second.

This is what happens during one simulated night:

  1. The fire starts and the alarm sounds. Everyone in the building hears it at once. The household in the burning flat reacts quickly.
  2. Everyone else waits for their reaction time, then starts to move. At night, many wait a long time.
  3. People move along the map. TailSafe moves them in small time steps, like the frames of a film. At every door and staircase it checks how many people can pass in that moment; the rest queue.
  4. Smoke spreads from the burning flat. It flows through open doors and into the corridors, and rises up the staircases and lift shafts, the way smoke rises up a chimney (this is called the stack effect). Doors that close by themselves slow it down. On some nights the burning flat's door is left open, which lets much more smoke out.
  5. Smoke slows people and harms them. Where it is dark people walk more slowly, and everyone keeps a count of how much smoke they have breathed.
  6. A staircase may fill with smoke at the time set in the scenario. From then on nobody can use it. People who reach it turn back and use another staircase. People already on it keep going.
  7. The firefighters start rescuing people who cannot leave on their own. The time they start differs from night to night. They go to the lowest floor where someone is waiting first, climb up, and bring the household down.
  8. The night ends when everyone is out. TailSafe follows the people who are moving for up to four hours after the alarm. Once only people waiting for the firefighters are left, it works out directly when each of them will be carried down, even if that is later still.

A 40-storey night takes the computer between a tenth and a third of a second, which is what makes hundreds or thousands of nights possible.

How the smoke is worked out

TailSafe's smoke model is deliberately simple. Each room, corridor, lobby and staircase is treated as one box of air, with one smoke level. Smoke flows from box to box through the openings between them, more easily upwards in stairs and lift shafts. The fire itself grows faster and faster until it reaches its full size; how fast it grows and how big it gets are rolled for every night. This is an engineering estimate, not a detailed computer fire simulation. Results from a detailed fire simulation program can be loaded into TailSafe instead.

Why it is called the “fast” simulator

The main simulator does not follow every footstep. It moves groups of people along the map's connections and keeps a queue at the end of each one, a bit like water flowing through pipes. Engineers call this a mesoscopic model (“meso” means “in the middle”: more detailed than a single equation, less detailed than following every step). TailSafe also has a slower, person-by-person simulator for closer looks, described in chapter 14.

In plain words

One simulated night is one possible version of the fire, played out second by second, with queues at every narrow place and smoke creeping through the building.

Chapter 10

Hundreds of nights

We never know exactly how a real fire night will go. So TailSafe does not play the night once: it plays it hundreds or thousands of times.

Each time, TailSafe “rolls the dice” again for everything that is uncertain. This way of working things out by repeating a random experiment many times is called the Monte Carlo method, after the famous casino.

Rolled again every night

  • who is at home, their ages, and whether a helper is there;
  • everyone's walking speed and reaction time;
  • which flat the fire starts in (and the floor, if you chose a random floor);
  • how fast the fire grows and how big it gets;
  • whether the burning flat's door is left open;
  • when a staircase is lost, if you gave a range of times;
  • which lifts are out of service;
  • when the firefighters start rescuing people.

Kept the same every night

  • the building;
  • everything you set on the scenario screen, such as the time of day, the share of older residents, the fire floor and a staircase smoke-logged at a fixed time;
  • any fix being tested, such as evacuation lifts or wardens.

How many nights?

You can choose 100, 300, 1,000 or 2,000 nights. More nights give more trustworthy answers but take longer. The browser version always uses 300. On a four-core computer, 1,000 nights of the 40-storey example take a little over a minute.

The seed: dice you can repeat

A computer's dice are not truly random. They come from a starting number called the seed. The same seed always gives exactly the same nights. That means anyone can repeat your study and get exactly the same numbers, and it means you can change one setting and be sure that everything else stayed the same.

Fair tests: the same nights for every plan

When TailSafe compares two plans, it runs both on the very same nights: the same people, the same fire, the same broken lift. Only the plan is different. It is like testing two umbrellas in the same rain, instead of one in a drizzle and the other in a storm. Statisticians call this common random numbers.

How sure are we? Confidence intervals

Every number worked out from a few hundred nights wobbles a little. Run another 300 nights with a different seed and you get a slightly different answer. So TailSafe gives each important number a range, the 95% confidence interval (CI): we can be fairly sure the true value lies inside it. The more nights, the narrower the range.

Watch out

With few nights, “the worst 5%” is only a handful of nights, and their average can mislead. For the time until everyone is out in the 40-storey example, the first 100 nights gave 206 minutes and all 1,000 nights gave 219. Use at least 300 nights to explore, and 1,000 or more for comparisons that matter.

Chapter 11

The numbers TailSafe reports

After a stress test, TailSafe reports a handful of numbers. This chapter explains each one.

Three ways to say “how long”

On screenWhat it measures
195% of occupantsThe time until 95 out of every 100 people are out. One or two very late people do not change it, so it shows how the crowd as a whole does.
2Last self-evacueeThe time until the last person who left on their own is out: on foot, carried by family, or by lift. It leaves out people waiting to be rescued, so it shows how the building works for people who can move.
3Everyone outThe time until the very last person is out, including people brought down by the firefighters. It depends heavily on how rescue works, which TailSafe can only estimate.

For each of the three, TailSafe gives the mean, the P95 and the CVaR95 (see chapter 3), each with its 95% confidence interval. Read them together: “everyone out” tells you about rescue, the other two about the stairs.

The smoke dose (FED)

As a person breathes smoke, TailSafe adds up a dose. The dose counts mainly carbon monoxide (a poisonous gas in smoke) and heat. Fire engineers call it the fractional effective dose, or FED.

  • At an FED of 1, a person is incapacitated: they can no longer move by themselves.
  • TailSafe treats an FED of 0.3 as already harmful, to protect the most vulnerable people. These limits follow ISO 13571.

P(RSET > ASET): how often someone is caught

This is the share of nights on which someone is still on a floor after its corridors have become untenable, or breathes a harmful dose of smoke (an FED of 0.3 or more). It is written as a number between 0 and 1: 0.60 means 60 nights in every 100. It needs the smoke to be simulated; without smoke it shows a dash.

Who carries the tail

On each of the worst nights, TailSafe looks at the late stragglers: the households still inside near the end of that night. It then compares the kinds of people among the stragglers with the kinds of people in the building, using a risk ratio:

risk ratio = share of the late stragglers ÷ share of all the people

A risk ratio of 1 means a group is just as common among the late stragglers as in the building. A risk ratio of 21 means it is 21 times more common. It also names the floors where the stragglers live, for example “wheelchair users on 30/F to 39/F”.

Floors that fail, and where the stairs queue

Floors that fail shows, for each floor, how often someone is still there after it has become untenable. Floors at and just above the fire are usually the worst. Where stairs queue shows how much queueing happens on each flight of each staircase on the worst nights, in person-minutes: if 10 people wait for 3 minutes, that is 30 person-minutes.

Chapter 12

Finding the cause

A bottleneck is a narrow place that slows everything behind it, like the neck of a bottle. TailSafe finds the bottlenecks that make the worst nights bad.

The obvious approach is to look for where the crowd is thickest. TailSafe goes one step further and asks a what-if question for every candidate: if this part of the building were better, how much shorter would the worst nights be? Asking what would have happened if something had been different is called a counterfactual test.

The candidates are:

  • each whole staircase, all its flights at once;
  • the doors of each staircase;
  • each final exit;
  • the places where the longest queues form on the worst nights;
  • any staircase the scenario blocks with smoke. Here the test is “what if it had stayed usable?”.

For each candidate, TailSafe gives it more room (50% more capacity by default; you can also choose 25% or 100%), re-runs the bad nights with exactly the same dice, and measures how much the average of the worst 5% falls. The candidates are then ranked, the biggest improvement first, each with its confidence interval.

TailSafe also works out two simpler facts about the building's escape routes:

  • Maximum egress flow: the most people per second the whole building could possibly get out, if everyone were already queued at the right places. In the 40-storey example it is 1.62 people per second, which is exactly two staircases' worth.
  • Minimum cut: the connections that set that limit, the narrowest point of the whole system. In the example it is the bottom flight of each staircase.
In plain words

TailSafe does not guess which door matters. It tries improving each one on the bad nights and measures the difference.

Chapter 13

Testing fixes

TailSafe looks for operational fixes: changes to how a building is run, not to its walls. They are cheap and quick compared with building work.

The five kinds of fix

FixWhat it meansThe catch
Evacuation liftsUse the ordinary (not the firefighting) lifts to carry residents who cannot manage the stairs: wheelchair users and frail older adults, or wheelchair users only. The lifts start after a short changeover and can serve the highest floors first, the nearest waiting floor, or the lowest floors first.People who wait for a lift may get out later than if they had walked.
Hold stair doors openKeep the staircase doors open, so more people can pass.Smoke also gets into the staircases more easily.
Stair assignment by floorTell some floors to use one staircase and the rest the other, so the streams do not collide. If the assigned staircase is blocked, people use whatever is quickest.A staircase can end up with too many people.
Phased releaseLet some floors leave first while others wait in their flats for a set time.People waiting in their flats still breathe any smoke that reaches them.
Floor wardensTrained residents or staff on a floor knock on every door of their floor and the floors next to it, so people react sooner, and each helps one household that cannot use the stairs alone.They have to be recruited and trained.

How TailSafe searches

There are far too many combinations to try them all, so TailSafe searches step by step, testing every candidate on the same set of nights:

1Try each fix on its ownFor example, lifts with each way of choosing floors, or a warden on each likely floor.
2Combine the good onesStart from the best single fix and add the others one at a time, as long as the result keeps getting better.
3Add wardensOne at a time, up to the number you allow.
4Fine-tune the timingIf letting floors leave in phases helped, adjust the waiting times. (The method used is called CMA-ES.)
5Confirm on fresh nightsTest the winning plan against doing nothing, on new nights it has never seen, and report only this result.

Why the fresh nights?

If you try 30 plans on the same 60 nights, the plan that looks best is partly lucky: it happens to suit those particular nights. This is called the winner's curse. It is like a student who memorised last year's exam: the real test is a new exam. So TailSafe tests the winning plan again on fresh nights (200 by default) and only then says whether it helps.

Better, worse, or no clear change

For every outcome, TailSafe shows the value without the plan, with the plan, the change, and the confidence interval of the change. Then it gives a verdict:

  • better: the whole range of the change is below zero;
  • worse: the whole range is above zero;
  • no clear change: the range includes zero, so we cannot tell.
Remember

A fix can make one thing better and another worse. In the 40-storey example, evacuation lifts halve the time until everyone is out, but make the time for 95% of people to get out several minutes longer. TailSafe always shows every outcome and warns you about trade-offs. It never hides them.

Chapter 14

Four helpers

Four more parts of TailSafe make the results easier to see, quicker to explore and easier to share.

1. The person-by-person replay

The fast simulator treats crowds like water in pipes. For a closer look, TailSafe has a second, microscopic simulator that moves every single person as a small disc across the floor plan, keeping a safe gap to whoever is in front (a collision-free speed model, after Tordeux, Chraibi and Seyfried, 2016). It is much slower, so TailSafe uses it only to replay single nights, and to check that the two simulators roughly agree. In a 20-storey block they agree on when the last walker leaves to within a few seconds on average; for most of the crowd, the detailed one is 7 to 15% slower, because real queues at doors take up room.

2. The quick guesser

A neural network is a program that learns patterns from examples. TailSafe's network has studied 800 simulated buildings and nights. Show it a building and some settings, and it estimates the results in a fraction of a second, instead of a minute or more. It passes messages along the building's map, from flat to corridor to staircase, which is why it is called a graph neural network. Its formal name is a surrogate: a stand-in for the simulator.

On buildings like the ones it learned from, its estimates of the bad nights are usually within 10 to 20% of the simulator. On a kind of building it has never seen, it can be off by a third or more.

Watch out

The quick guesser learned from the simulator, not from real fires, so it can only be as good as the simulator, and usually a little worse. It warns you when your settings are outside what it has learned. Before you rely on a number, press Confirm with full simulation.

3. The briefing

A one-page summary for a building manager, which can be saved as a PDF. TailSafe writes it by filling fixed sentences with the numbers from your results, and then checks every number in the text against the results. Whoever runs the full app can also switch on an optional AI writer (a large language model) to draft the text. It is off unless it is set up. If its draft contains any number that is not in the results, the draft is thrown away and the fixed-sentence briefing is shown instead.

4. The floor-plan reader

It turns a picture of a floor plan into a TailSafe building. It finds the walls, rooms, doorways and staircases in the picture; you correct its mistakes; then it stacks the floor into a tower. It works in the full app only. Chapter 29 shows how to use it.

Chapter 17

The full app with Docker, step by step

Docker is a free program that runs software inside a container: a sealed box that holds everything the software needs. With Docker you do not have to install Python or anything else yourself.

Step 1. Install Docker (once)

  • Windows or Mac: download Docker Desktop from https://www.docker.com/products/docker-desktop/ and install it. On a Mac, choose the version for your chip (Apple or Intel). The installer may ask you to restart the computer. Then open Docker Desktop and wait until it says that Docker is running.
  • Linux: install Docker Engine by following the steps for your system at https://docs.docker.com/engine/install/.

Step 2. Download TailSafe (once)

  • If you know git:
    git clone https://github.com/chingkheinganba231005/Tail_Safe.git
  • If you do not: open https://github.com/chingkheinganba231005/Tail_Safe, click the green Code button, then Download ZIP. Unzip the file. You get a folder called Tail_Safe-main.

Step 3. Open a terminal in the TailSafe folder

A terminal is a window where you type commands.

  • Windows: open the TailSafe folder in File Explorer, click the address bar at the top, type powershell and press Enter.
  • Mac: open Terminal (in Applications, then Utilities), type cd followed by a space, drag the TailSafe folder into the window, and press Enter.
  • Linux: right-click inside the folder and choose Open in Terminal.

Step 4. Build TailSafe (once, and after each update)

docker build -t tailsafe .

Type it exactly, including the full stop at the end, which means “this folder”. The first build downloads a lot of software and can take several minutes. Later builds are quicker, because Docker remembers the parts that have not changed.

Step 5. Start TailSafe

docker run -p 7860:7860 tailsafe

After a few seconds a line like Uvicorn running on http://0.0.0.0:7860 appears. TailSafe is now running. Leave this window open.

Step 6. Open it

In your web browser go to http://localhost:7860. localhost means “this computer”, and 7860 is the port: a numbered door on your computer where TailSafe is listening. The label under the black bar now says PROTOTYPE instead of BROWSER VERSION, and every option works.

Step 7 (optional). Use it from a phone or tablet

  1. Make sure the phone and the computer are on the same Wi-Fi network.
  2. Find the computer's IP address (its address on your network), for example 192.168.1.23.
    • Windows: in PowerShell type ipconfig and look for IPv4 Address.
    • Mac: System Settings, then Wi-Fi, then Details next to your network.
    • Linux: type hostname -I and use the first address.
  3. On the phone, open http://192.168.1.23:7860, using your computer's address.
  4. If it does not load, the computer's firewall may be blocking it. Allow Docker through the firewall when asked, and check the address again.

Step 8. Stop it

Press Ctrl and C together in the terminal window, or stop the container in Docker Desktop. To start again later, repeat step 5; you do not need to build again.

Good to know
  • The first simulation is slower. After each start, the simulator needs about ten seconds to prepare itself the first time it runs.
  • How long runs take. On a four-core computer, 300 nights of the 40-storey example take about 20 seconds and 1,000 nights a little over a minute. Ranking bottlenecks and searching for a plan re-run many nights, so they take several minutes.
  • Repeats are instant. While TailSafe is running it remembers results, so asking for the same thing twice gives the answer at once. When you stop the container, it forgets.
  • To update TailSafe, download it again (or run git pull in the folder), then build again (step 4).

  3. Scenarios per plan: how many nights each candidate is tested on while searching (30, 60, 100 or 200). Confirmation scenarios: how many fresh nights the winner is checked on (100, 200 or 400). More nights are more reliable but slower.
  4. Levers the plan may use: untick any fix that is not possible in your building.
  5. Click Find a better plan. In the full app this can take several minutes.

In the browser version, the recorded search used the standard settings: the tail of the time until everyone is out, 60 nights per plan, 200 fresh nights, at most 2 wardens, all five fixes.

Reading the result

Here the plan is to use the non-firefighting lifts for mobility-impaired residents (nearest waiting floor first) and to station floor wardens on 14/F and 35/F. On the fresh nights:

  • the time until everyone is out drops from 201.5 to 102.0 minutes: better;
  • the time until the last self-evacuee is out drops from 108.3 to 102.0 minutes: better;
  • the time for 95% of occupants rises from 66.8 to 75.4 minutes: worse;
  • P(RSET > ASET) drops from 0.58 to 0.55: better.

The orange Trade-off warning points out the outcome that got worse. Frail residents who would otherwise have walked now wait for the lifts, and on nights when one of the evacuation lifts is broken, the other cannot keep up.

Reading a floor-plan image

No building file? The full app can turn a picture of a typical floor plan into a TailSafe building. You help it by correcting what it gets wrong.

Do this

  2. Choose a file: a PNG or JPEG picture, or a PDF (TailSafe uses its first page). Or click Try a sample plan to practise.
  3. 1. Scale. Click the Reference line tool, then click both ends of something whose length you know, such as a 10 m grid line. Type its real length in the box. Without a line, TailSafe guesses the scale from the thickness of the walls, which is less reliable.
  4. Click 2. Read plan. TailSafe finds the rooms and doorways and colours the rooms by type.
  5. 3. Check and correct. With the Select tool, click a room and change its type in the menu: Flat / room, Corridor, Lobby, Staircase, Refuge area or Not walkable. Warnings in orange tell you what to check.
  6. 4. Build. Type the number of storeys (including G/F) and a name, then click Build building →. The same plan is used for every floor, and doorways to the outside become exits on the ground floor.
  7. Check the new building on the usual step 1 screen, then click Confirm building →.

What the reader can and cannot do

  • Walls must run straight across and straight up and down the picture. Turn scanned plans upright first; curved or slanted walls are not read.
  • Walls must be drawn thicker than other lines. Plans drawn with thin or hatched walls need more correcting.
  • Doorways are gaps between 0.6 and 2.0 m wide. Wider openings join the rooms on both sides into one.
  • Refuge floors and lifts are not found; the ground floor usually differs from the typical floor and should be checked.
  • If the plan shows no doors to the outside, TailSafe puts an exit at the foot of every staircase, and says so.
  • On TailSafe's own test plans it finds 96 to 100% of the doorways. Those plans are drawn by the computer, so real drawings will do worse: checking is part of the job.

The plan is only read on the computer running the full app; it is not sent anywhere else.

Chapter 30

A worked example: one night at 3 a.m.

This chapter follows one question all the way through TailSafe, using a larger study of 1,000 nights run in the full app.

The question

A 40-storey public housing block. It is Sunday, 3 a.m. A fire starts in a flat on 14/F. Four minutes later, Stair A fills with smoke. One lift is out of service. 22% of the residents are 65 or older. How bad could the evacuation get, why, and what would help?

This is TailSafe's reference scenario: the standard example that the browser version records for every building type, and that the command line uses unless you give it another one. The numbers below come from 1,000 nights with seed 0. The browser version's 300 nights are the first 300 of these, so its numbers are close but not identical.

1. How bad is it?

  • On an average night, 95% of the residents are out after about an hour (60 minutes); on the worst 5% of nights, after 66.5 minutes. The last person able to leave unaided is out after 86 minutes on average.
  • Everyone is out, counting the people carried down by firefighters, after 138 minutes on average and 219 minutes on the worst 5% of nights. The gap between the two shows that a few bad nights hide behind the average.
  • Smoke catches someone on 60% of nights, most often on 14/F (58% of nights), 15/F (47%) and 16/F (19%): the fire floor and the floors just above it. On 6.5% of nights at least one person is incapacitated.

2. Who is left?

  • Households with a wheelchair user on 30/F to 39/F are 1.2% of the residents but all of the people still inside at the very end of the worst nights. They wait for the firefighters, who come down the stairs one household at a time.
  • Among people leaving on their own, households with a frail resident aged 80 or over on 30/F to 39/F are 4.9% of the residents but 70% of the late stragglers.

3. Why?

Losing Stair A. From four minutes on, the whole block must use Stair B, which lets about 48 people a minute down. Queues form on Stair B on every bad night. The bottleneck ranking confirms it: keeping Stair A usable would cut the worst-5% average of the time for 95% of residents to get out by 12.8 minutes (12.2 to 13.4), and giving Stair B 50% more capacity by 8.6 minutes. Wider doors would hardly matter.

4. What helps?

The plan search proposes using the non-firefighting lifts for mobility-impaired residents (nearest waiting floor first), and floor wardens on 14/F and 35/F. Checked on 400 fresh nights:

5. What should a building manager take away?

  1. The longest nights are about rescue. Residents who cannot use the stairs wait a long time. Lifts for them halve the tail of the time until everyone is out.
  2. But there is a price. With lifts, frail residents who could have walked wait for a lift instead, so the crowd as a whole gets out later. When one of the evacuation lifts is the broken one, the other cannot keep up. This needs weighing, perhaps by limiting the lifts to wheelchair users (a setting TailSafe can test).
  3. Stair A matters most. Anything that keeps it usable for longer is worth discussing with a fire engineer.
  4. All of this rests on assumed numbers (chapter 32). It shows where to look and what to ask, not exact minutes.

Your own scenario in a file

The stress test, the plan search and the replays take --spec followed by a scenario file, written in YAML (a simple text format) or JSON. For example, save this as my-night.yaml:

name: weekday-afternoon
time_slot: weekday_day         # or weekday_night, weekend_day, weekend_night
share_65_plus: 0.30            # 30% of residents aged 65 or over
fire_level: 10                 # fire on 10/F
stair_blockages:
  - stair: B                   # Stair B lost between 2 and 10 minutes in

hazard: {enabled: true}        # simulate the smoke

and run it with tailsafe stress run cruciform --spec my-night.yaml --runs 300 --out out/weekday. Times are in seconds. Anything you leave out takes its standard value.

Chapter 32

Limits and responsible use

Every model simplifies the world. These are the most important simplifications in TailSafe, and they are why its results are for learning and comparing, not for exact answers.

  • Many numbers are educated guesses. 146 of the 169 numbers in the settings file are marked as assumptions, including how fast frail people walk, how long people take to react at night, and how quickly firefighters rescue people.
  • The buildings are generic. The four building types are made by rules, not measured from real blocks. Real stair widths, door widths and refuge floors may differ.
  • The smoke model is simple: one fire; each room is one well-mixed box of air; no wind, no sprinklers, no fans that keep smoke out of the stairs. It is not a detailed fire simulation.
  • People behave simply. Everyone knows the quickest way out, and only learns a staircase is blocked when they reach it. On the stairs, fast walkers can pass slow ones freely, which is optimistic on narrow stairs.
  • Rescue is a rough model. It is an assumption, and it decides how long “everyone out” takes. Always read it alongside the other two outcomes.
  • Not yet compared with real drills. TailSafe's checks show that the simulator does what the engineering method says. They do not show that the method and the numbers are right for Hong Kong towers.
  • The quick guesser learns the simulator, not reality, and is weakest for building types unlike those it learned from, especially care homes.
  • The browser version uses 300 nights, so its confidence intervals are wider than those of a 1,000-night study.

The full list is in docs/assumptions.md, and the checks are described in docs/validation.md.

How to talk about the results

Do not say

  • “Our building is safe.”
  • “Evacuation takes 219 minutes.”
  • “TailSafe proved that lifts are the answer.”

Say instead

  • “In TailSafe's model, the worst nights come from losing Stair A.”
  • “The model suggests lifts help wheelchair users a lot, with a cost for the crowd.”
  • “We should ask a fire engineer about keeping Stair A usable.”

Chapter 33

Troubleshooting

Something not working? Find the problem below.

In the web app

A message says the browser version has no results for these settings.

The browser version only holds the reference scenario of each building. Press Back to the reference scenario, use the What-if screen for an estimate, or run the full app (chapter 17).

A step in the list is grey and cannot be clicked.

Do the earlier steps first. Most steps need a building (step 1), and the results need a stress test (step 2).

The 3D view is blank.

Your browser may have WebGL, its graphics feature, switched off. Update the browser, try another one, or turn on “hardware acceleration” in its settings.

The charts are cramped on my phone.

Turn the phone sideways, or switch the chart to Table.

The What-if screen shows an orange box “Outside what the surrogate was trained on”.

Your settings go beyond what the quick guesser learned. Treat its estimate as rough and press Confirm with full simulation (in the full app).

The briefing shows a note that a draft was rejected.

The optional AI writer used a number that is not in the results, so TailSafe showed the fixed-sentence briefing instead. Nothing needs fixing.

The floor-plan reader misses rooms or joins them.

Draw a reference line for the scale, check that the walls are thick and straight, then fix room types by hand and add missing doorways (chapter 29).

With the full app

“docker: command not found”, or “Cannot connect to the Docker daemon”.

Docker is not installed, or not running. Open Docker Desktop and wait until it says it is running, then try again.

“port is already allocated”.

Something else, perhaps another copy of TailSafe, is already using port 7860. Stop it, or start TailSafe on another port with docker run -p 8080:7860 tailsafe and open http://localhost:8080.

The build stops with a download or certificate error.

Some networks, often at work, block or inspect downloads. Try another network, or ask your IT team.

My phone cannot open the full app.

Use the computer's IP address, not localhost (which on the phone means the phone itself). Check that both are on the same Wi-Fi, and allow Docker through the computer's firewall.

The first run is slow.

That is normal: the simulator prepares itself the first time (about ten seconds).

A run takes very long.

Use fewer nights (300 instead of 2,000). Ranking bottlenecks and searching for a plan are the slowest steps; fewer scenarios per plan makes the search quicker.

My numbers differ from someone else's.

Compare the seed, the number of nights, the building options and every scenario setting. The same settings with the same seed always give the same numbers.

Chapter 34

Questions people ask

Does TailSafe use real residents' data?

No. Every resident is made up by the computer, following general rules about ages, households and behaviour. No personal data is used.

Can TailSafe tell me whether my building is safe?

No. It is a learning and decision-support tool. Only a registered fire engineer, the Fire Services Department and compliance with the Code of Practice can do that.

Is anything I enter sent somewhere?

In the browser version, nothing you enter is uploaded. In the full app, buildings and floor plans go only to the computer that runs it; on a public Hugging Face Space, that is Hugging Face's server.

Why does “everyone out” take hours?

Because a few residents cannot use the stairs and wait for the firefighters, who, in TailSafe's model, rescue one household at a time. How rescue works is an assumption. The other two outcomes show how the stairs themselves perform.

Why are the lifts not used?

In Hong Kong, lifts are normally kept for the firefighters during a fire, so TailSafe's standard scenario does not use them. Using some lifts to evacuate residents who cannot manage the stairs is one of the fixes you can test.

Why did my results change when I changed the seed?

A different seed means different dice, so different nights. With enough nights the change is small, and the confidence intervals tell you how small.

How many nights should I run?

300 to explore; 1,000 or more for comparisons that matter.

Why is the What-if estimate different from the full simulation?

It is a quick learned guess. On buildings like those it trained on it is usually within 10 to 20%; on unfamiliar buildings it can be further off. That is why the confirm button exists.

Can I use TailSafe for an office, a school or a shopping centre?

Not as it is. TailSafe is built for homes: households, who is at home at different times of day, and residents' ages. Other buildings would need a different model of the people in them.

Where do TailSafe's numbers come from?

From one settings file, config/params.yaml. Every number there says where it comes from, or that it is still an assumption. tailsafe params list --assumptions lists the assumptions.

Where do I report a problem or suggest an idea?

On the project's GitHub page, github.com/chingkheinganba231005/Tail_Safe, under Issues.
